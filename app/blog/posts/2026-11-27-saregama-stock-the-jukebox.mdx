That's Saregama on one page. Below is the same story in words, built from thirteen calls, the investor decks since Q3 FY24, three annual reports and six broker notes.

Picture a jukebox in every café in the country. Each record in it was paid for once, and every play drops a coin. A good record earns back its cost in a few years and keeps paying for decades. But the jukebox owner does not own the cafés: they decide what a play is worth, and the best new records go to whoever bids the most.

Saregama owns the records: more than 180,000 Indian songs, recorded since 1902, paid for each time they are streamed, watched or played on air. For years it also made films and ran concerts, which pay once, and only if they work. Between December 2025 and August 2026 it chose: a stake in a famous filmmaker whose music now comes to it, no more films of its own, and the money into new records.

Where this lives on the portal: the [Saregama company page](/company/SAREGAMA).

## 1. What Saregama makes

### A catalogue of 180,000 songs, paid by the play

Saregama licenses its songs to streaming services, YouTube, short-video apps, broadcasters and brands, and adds 4,500 to 5,000 new releases a year. A recording is protected for 60 years from release, and the lyrics and tune for 60 years after the writer and composer die. In FY26, 60% of music revenue came from songs released after 2000, so most of what earns today has decades left to run.

### Music is 83% of the business

Music, which covers licensing, managing artistes and Carvaan music players, was 83% of FY26 revenue, at an EBITDA margin of about 63% before the cost of new songs is charged off. Video (films, TV and web series) was 11% and barely profitable; live events were 6% and lost ₹7.1 Cr.

### The cafés set the price of a play

Management ranks Saregama second to T-Series by music revenue. Short-video apps pay each label a fixed fee on one-year deals; Saregama wants a share of their advertising and does not have it yet.

## 2. How it got here

### From the Gramophone Company to the Goenka group

The story starts in 1902, with a recording of Gauhar Jaan, the first Indian song on record. The company was incorporated in 1946 as The Gramophone Company (India), and the RP-Sanjiv Goenka group acquired it in 1986.

### Side bets on films, concerts and a digital studio

Around the music it built other businesses: live events from 2022, control of the digital studio Pocket Aces in 2023, and Diljit Dosanjh's India tour in Q3 FY25, which brought ₹279 Cr of event revenue in one quarter.

### Then the turn to songs

In December 2025 Saregama agreed to pay ₹325 Cr for preference shares in Bhansali Productions that convert into a 28% to 49.9% stake; the studio sends all its future film music to Saregama. The deal closed on 30 January 2026. In May 2026 Saregama cut the capital video and live events may use from 18% to mid-single digits, and in August it said it would wind down its own films and put its film money through Bhansali.

Two purchases sit inside the music numbers that follow: the NAV Records Haryanvi catalogue, part of ₹105 Cr of catalogue purchases in FY26, and Finnet Media, an artiste business Pocket Aces acquired in November 2025. The split between bought and organic growth is not disclosed.

## 3. How the numbers have moved

### One tour swung the revenue; music kept climbing

Revenue was ₹803 Cr in FY24. In FY25 it jumped to ₹1,171 Cr, mostly on the Diljit tour, booked at full ticket value. In FY26 the tour did not repeat and video fell 44%, so revenue fell 16% to ₹985 Cr. Music grew through all of it: ₹674 Cr in FY24, ₹694 Cr in FY25 and ₹814 Cr in FY26, up 17%.

### The margin follows the mix

Operating margin was 35% in FY22, 30-31% as films and concerts grew, 24% in the tour year and 34% in FY26, when music was 83% of revenue again. Concerts and films keep little of each rupee they bring in; songs already owned keep most of it.

### Profit stood still while the song bill rose

Net profit went from ₹198 Cr in FY24 to ₹206 Cr in FY26. New songs are paid for up front and charged off over up to ten years, and spending on them rose from ₹110 Cr in FY22 to ₹316 Cr in FY25; interest income fell as the cash from a 2021 share placement was spent. Return on capital slid from 23% in FY22 to 17%, though music alone earned about 33% on its assets. Operating cash was ₹100 Cr in FY26 against ₹206 Cr of profit. The checks read clean: an unqualified audit, no promoter pledge, related-party trade at 7.0% of revenue.

### Copyright protects the old records, not the new ones

Our moat call is narrow, and it rests on one thing: Saregama owns the copyright on its catalogue. Nothing else holds. Platforms license every label, so leaving costs them nothing, and new releases are won at auction against T-Series, Sony, Universal and Warner.

## 4. The last few quarters

### Music grew 35%, part of it bought

Early FY26 was soft after Airtel shut its free Wynk Music app: music grew 5% in Q1 FY26 and 10% in Q2. Then growth jumped: 22% in Q3, 30% in Q4, 39% in Q1 FY27 and 35% in Q2 FY27. About ₹25 Cr of Q2's ₹65 Cr increase came from artiste management, which nearly doubled with Finnet's artistes included; licensing and retail grew about 25%.

### Films shrinking, concerts growing at a loss

Q2 FY27 revenue was ₹314 Cr, up 37%. Video fell 16% and lost ₹7.5 Cr in the half; live events doubled to ₹45 Cr but lost ₹2.8 Cr, as Saregama expenses the launch costs of its own festivals.

### The margin is holding, not widening

After the cost of new songs, music kept 46% of its revenue over the twelve months to September 2026, as a year earlier. Licensing's margin rose as older songs paid back, management says, but artiste management, at about 10%, grew to a fifth of music revenue and absorbed the gain.

### Customers' advances paid for this half's songs

In H1 FY27, by the investor deck's cash-flow table, operating cash of ₹286 Cr covered ₹185 Cr of spending on new content, but ₹112 Cr of it came from working capital, mostly customer advances. Without that, it would have been about ₹174 Cr, just short of the bill.

## 5. Where growth comes from next

### Love & War and the Bhansali albums

The Bhansali deal secures Saregama's A and B+ Hindi film albums for 24 to 30 months from May 2026, about 30% and possibly 40% of its Hindi film music. The first big one, Love & War, is due in January 2027.

### More records each year, at a slower rise

The FY27 budget for new music is ₹300-350 Cr, Bhansali's music included, and FY28 to FY30 is guided at about ₹1,200 Cr combined, rising about 10% a year. Each year's block is meant to pay back in about five years and then earn for decades.

### Our base case: 18-23% a year

Management guides only music: 20-23% a year over three to five years, at a 60-65% EBITDA margin. Our base case for the whole company, on consolidated numbers, is 18-23% a year over two years: music at or above its guide, video shrinking, events lumpy. The bull case is 24-29% and the bear case 11-16%. We hold the margin flat, as no company margin is guided.

### The number that decides it

It is music's margin after the cost of new songs, 46% today. Management expects it to improve by 3-5% over three to five years as older blocks pay back, without saying whether that means points.

## 6. What management promised, and what it delivered

The record is credible. Saregama has met 4 of its 6 closed financial guides, and its dates mostly hold: 6 slips across 5 of 27 dated threads.

The miss that mattered was FY25 music. Licensing and artiste management were guided to grow at least 25-26%, cut to 23-24% in November 2024, and grew about 12%, from ₹544 Cr to ₹610 Cr: about 11 points short, half the guided rate. The video margin missed too, 1.4% against 8-10% guided for FY26.

What it kept: FY26 music growth, cut twice to 17-18%, came in at 22% on the same basis, and the company's own adjusted EBITDA margin came in at 41% against a 32-33% guide. The worst slip is Love & War, first set for March 2026 and now due in January 2027, ten months after its first date.

The company-wide targets are further off: profit before tax, guided in February 2024 to double in three to four years, is up 5% two years on, from ₹271 Cr to ₹284 Cr.

## 7. What the price already assumes

At ₹621 on 26 November, Saregama is worth about ₹11,973 Cr, at 48.1 times trailing earnings against a five-year median of 43.5. A reverse DCF on that price, holding margins flat, implies about 41% revenue growth a year over the next couple of years.

Restated from the trailing twelve months, which already carry the first half's jump, our base case is about 17% a year and the bull case about 25%. The price asks for about 16 points a year more than the bull case. Revenue has grown 17% a year over five years, and the last two quarters grew 27.5% and 36.5%.

## What would make me wrong

- **Music is already running above the band.** H1 FY27 music grew 37%; if the Bhansali albums land on time, a couple of years well above 20-23% is possible.
- **The margin could widen.** Song spending is guided to rise about 10% a year, half the music guide's pace. If older blocks pay back, profit outgrows revenue and our flat-margin cases undercount it.
- **Pricing could arrive.** A share of short-video advertising, or paid subscriptions lifting what a play earns, would show pricing power our moat call does not allow for.

The opposite error would be to read H1's 37% as the run rate: it came off a soft base, and part of it was bought.

A jukebox earns a coin on every play once the record has paid for itself. Saregama has stopped making films to stock the jukebox, and the price assumes the coins pile up faster than even our bull case.
